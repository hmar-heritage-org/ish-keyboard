# ISH Standard: Justification for Orthographic Morpheme Separation
### Particles *Hai*, *-a*, *-ah*, and the Derivational *-na* vs. Stative *Na* Distinction

**Document Version:** 1.1  
**Author / Standard:** Hmar Heritage Foundation — ISH (Inhmangrêm Siemṭhat Hmar) Orthography Standard  
**Status:** Working Proposal / Draft Appendix for Master ISH Standard  

---

## 1. Executive Summary & Core Principle

In legacy Hmar orthography, writers frequently attached free grammatical particles (such as the plural marker `hai`, the locative `ah`, and the pronominal/locative clitic `a`) directly to the preceding noun or root as bound suffixes (e.g., `*lekhabu-hai` $\to$ `*lekhabuhai`, `*ram-ah` $\to$ `*ramah`, `*pang-a` $\to$ `*panga`). 

The **ISH Standard** strictly formalizes that **these markers are free syntactic particles and clitics operating at the phrasal level, NOT bound inflectional affixes.** Consequently, they must **always be separated by a space** from the preceding noun:

$$\text{Noun / Phrase} + \text{[Space]} + \mathbf{hai} \mid \mathbf{ah} \mid \mathbf{a}$$

Treating these particles as fused suffixes is grammatically unsound and introduces catastrophic semantic ambiguity and lexical collisions (e.g., homographic clash with unitary roots like `panga` = five, `luhai` = dizziness, and `theihai` = mango). Conversely, bound derivational affixes (such as the nominalizer `-na` in `hmangaina`) must remain fused.

---

## 2. Definitive Proof & Minimal Pairs: Semantic Collisions

The primary empirical argument against fusing `hai` onto nouns is that **`hai` forms distinct monosemic lexical compounds in Hmar**. When plural `hai` is fused, identical homographic collisions occur that obscure meaning and paralyze computational parsing and natural reading.

### Minimal Pair 1: *Theihai* vs. *Thei hai*

| Written Form | Morphological Structure | Part of Speech | Exact Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`theihai`** | `thei` + `hai` (Single Lexeme) | Noun (Singular) | **Mango** (*Mangifera indica*) |
| **`thei hai`** | `thei` (fruit) + `hai` (plural marker) | Noun Phrase (Plural) | **Fruits** (Various kinds of fruit) |

- **The Problem:** If plural `hai` were attached directly to nouns, writing "fruits" as `*theihai` would make it completely indistinguishable from a single "mango".
- **Plural Formation Under ISH:**
  - One mango: `theihai`
  - Multiple mangoes: **`theihai hai`** (*the mangoes*)
  - Multiple fruits: **`thei hai`** (*the fruits*)

---

### Minimal Pair 2: *Luhai* vs. *Lu hai*

| Written Form | Morphological Structure | Part of Speech | Exact Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`luhai`** | `lu` + `hai` (Compound Stative/Noun) | Noun / Adjective | **Dizziness / Vertigo / Lightheadedness** (Head spinning) |
| **`lu hai`** | `lu` (head) + `hai` (plural marker) | Noun Phrase (Plural) | **Heads** (Anatomical heads) |

- **The Problem:** Fusing `hai` onto `lu` (head) produces `*luhai`, meaning "dizziness" or "vertigo", rather than the intended plural "heads".
- **Plural Formation Under ISH:**
  - Plural of head: **`lu hai`** (*the heads*)
  - Condition of dizziness: `luhai`
  - Plural of dizzy individuals: **`luhai hai`** (*dizzy people / individuals experiencing vertigo*)

---

### Minimal Pair 3: Polysemic Tri-Split Ambiguity (*Hmangaihaia*)

Fusing particles and morphemes does not just cause simple binary collisions—it can create a 3-way or 4-way semantic impasse where readers and machine parsers cannot deduce the author's intent without syntax analysis:

| Written Form (ISH Standard) | Underlying Morphemes | Exact Meaning | Context / Usage |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`hmang hai a`** | `hmang` (user / use) + `hai` (plural) + `a` (locative) | **Among the users / in using** | *"A hmang hai a chun buoina a um nawh"* |
| **`hma ngai a`** | `hma` (future / forward) + `ngai` (care / long for) + `a` (adverbial) | **Longing for / considering the future** | *"Hma ngai a hma la ding"* |
| **`hmangai hai a`** | `hmangai` (beloved / loved ones) + `hai` (plural) + `a` (locative) | **Among those loved / among the beloved** | *"A hmangai hai a chun a lawm"* |
| **`hmangai a`** | `hmangai` (to love) + `a` (conjunction / particle) | **To love and / loving** | *"Hmangai a rawngbawl"* |

- **The Problem:** When written in legacy fused orthography as `*hmangaihaia`, the reader is forced to pause and mentally parse whether the text refers to:
  1. *The users* (`hmang hai a`),
  2. *Caring for the future* (`hma ngai a`), or
  3. *Those who are loved* (`hmangai hai a`).
- **Resolution Under ISH:** By strictly delimiting morpheme boundaries with spaces, every intended grammatical and semantic distinction is immediately clear, transparent, and unambiguous.

---

### Minimal Pair 4: Numeral *Panga* (5) vs. Postpositional Clitic *Pang a* (On the Body / Side)

| Written Form | Morphological Structure | Part of Speech | Exact Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`panga`** | `pa-` (archaic numeral prefix) + `nga` (root) | Numeral (Cardinal) | **Five (5)** (Unitary cardinal number) |
| **`pang a`** | `pang` (body / side / flank) + `a` (pronoun / locative clitic) | Noun Phrase + Postposition | **On the body / at his/her side / in the flank** |

- **The Problem:** In legacy orthography, writers frequently attached the locative or pronominal clitic `a` directly onto preceding nouns (e.g., writing `*panga` for *"on the body"* or *"at his side"*). This produces an immediate homographic collision with the cardinal numeral `panga` (*"five"*):
  - *Legacy Fused Ambiguity:* *"A panga vaw"* $\to$ Does it mean *"Strike him five times"* or *"Strike him on his body/side"*?
  - *Legacy Fused Ambiguity:* *"In panga chun"* $\to$ Does it mean *"In five houses"* or *"At the side of the house"*?
- **Resolution Under ISH:**
  - Cardinal numeral 5: **`panga`** (always written as a single unitary word).
  - Anatomical / spatial clitic phrase: **`pang a`** (strictly separated with a space).
  - Example: *"A pang a lum"* (*"His body is warm"*), *"In pang a chun"* (*"At the side of the house"*).
  - Splitting `a` as an autonomous pronoun/clitic eliminates ambiguity across thousands of Hmar words while protecting the inviolate numeral root `panga`.

### Minimal Pair 5: Onomastic Anthroponyms (Names) vs. Syntactic Clitics (*Lawma*, *Rama*, *Thanga*, *Hlima*, *Runa*, *Fela*)

In Hmar cultural onomastics, male personal names are formed by suffixing the masculine onomastic marker `-a` to lexical roots denoting virtues, aspirations, geography, or states of being.

| Root Meaning | Derived Personal Name (TitleCase) | Fused Legacy Lowercase | ISH Standard Decoupled Form | Syntactic Meaning in Running Text |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`lawm`** (grateful / joy / rejoice) | **`Lawma`** (Personal Name) | `*lawma` | **`lawm a`** | *"In joy / with gratitude / rejoicing and..."* |
| **`ram`** (land / country / jungle / forest) | **`Rama`** (Personal Name) | `*rama` | **`ram a`** | *"In the country / into the jungle / to the land"* (Locative) |
| **`thang`** (fame / renown / spread) | **`Thanga`** (Personal Name) | `*thanga` | **`thang a`** | *"In fame / renowned and / his fame..."* |
| **`hlim`** (happy / glad / joyful) | **`Hlima`** (Personal Name) | `*hlima` | **`hlim a`** | *"In happiness / with gladness / joyful and..."* |
| **`run`** (home / hearth / dwelling / abode) | **`Runa`** (Personal Name) | `*runa` | **`run a`** | *"At the hearth / in the home / into the abode"* (Locative) |
| **`fel`** (good / righteous / upright / kind) | **`Fela`** (Personal Name) | `*fela` | **`fel a`** | *"With righteousness / upright and / being kind..."* (Adverbial) |

#### The Catastrophic Ambiguity in Legacy Writing
In legacy fused orthography, attaching the grammatical clitic `a` directly onto common nouns and verbs creates crippling homographic collisions with proper names:
1. **Semantic Collision:** When a sentence begins or contains `*rama hung a...`, does it mean:
   - *"Rama came and..."* (Proper Noun Subject), or
   - *"Coming into the land / forest..."* (Locative Adverbial Clause)?
2. **Failure of Natural Language Processing (NLP):**
   - For computational parsers, Part-of-Speech (POS) taggers, and Named Entity Recognition (NER) models, fusing `a` causes pervasive false positives. The model cannot distinguish a person's identity from a spatial postposition or verbal conjunction without costly contextual disambiguation.
   - For LLM tokenization, training on fused forms causes token fragmentation and blends the semantic vector of an individual (`Rama`) with the geographical concept of land (`ram`) and locative case (`a`).

#### The Resolution Under the ISH Standard
The ISH Standard establishes an absolute, deterministic boundary:
1. **Proper Names are Strictly Capitalized (TitleCase):** Names like **`Lawma`**, **`Rama`**, **`Thanga`**, **`Hlima`**, **`Runa`**, and **`Fela`** are self-justifying as single, capitalized proper nouns. They are cataloged in a dedicated onomastic lexicon ([`hmar_anthroponyms.tsv`](file:///home/phxlm/Work/HHF/ISH-key/resources/lexicon/hmar_anthroponyms.tsv)) and are never lowercased or decomposed.
2. **Syntactic Clitics in Lowercase are Strictly Separated:** When the clitic `a` functions grammatically (as a pronoun, locative marker, or conjunction), it **must be preceded by a space**: **`lawm a`**, **`ram a`**, **`thang a`**, **`hlim a`**, **`run a`**, **`fel a`**.
3. **Keyboard Implementation:** Typing lowercase `rama` or `lawma` immediately triggers the decoupled suggestion `ram a` and `lawm a` via [`fused_map.json`](file:///home/phxlm/Work/HHF/ISH-key/android/app/src/main/assets/models/fused_map.json), while typing capitalized `Rama` or `Lawma` suggests the intact personal name.

This ensures **100% mutual exclusivity** between proper names and syntactic phrases.

## 3. Derivational Nominalizer *-na* vs. Autonomous Stative Lexeme *Na*

A frequent question in Hmar orthography concerns the element **`na`**:
- When is `na` a **bound derivational suffix** (written fused)?
- When is `na` an **independent lexical word** (written separated)?
- Why is dizziness fused as **`luhai`**, whereas headache is separated as **`lu na`**?

### 3.1 What is *-na*? (The Derivational Nominalizer)
When attached to verbs or predicates, **`-na` is a bound Derivational Nominalizing Suffix**. 
Its grammatical role is to convert a verb/action into an **abstract noun**, a **conceptual state**, or a **gerundive essence/act**:

| Base Verb | Part of Speech | Derived Form | Exact Meaning | Function of *-na* |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`hmangai`** | Verb (to love) | **`hmangaina`** | **Love** (The act, virtue, and concept of loving) | Derivational Nominalizer |
| **`nuom`** | Verb (to want, desire) | **`nuomna`** | **Desire / Will / Willingness** (The act/state of wanting) | Derivational Nominalizer |
| **`dam`** | Verb (to be healthy/well) | **`damna`** | **Health / Healing / Cure** | Derivational Nominalizer |
| **`hring`** | Verb (to live) | **`hringna`** | **Life** | Derivational Nominalizer |
| **`thawk`** | Verb (to work) | **`thawkna`** | **Workplace / Labor / Instrument** | Nominalizer of Instrument/Place |
| **`chhandam`** | Verb (to save / rescue) | **`chhandamna`** | **Salvation** | Derivational Nominalizer |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Orthographic Rule:** Because derivational affixes alter the grammatical category of the root (Verb $\to$ Noun) and forge a single new semantic concept in the lexicon, all **nominalizations with `-na` must ALWAYS be written fused as a single unitary word** (`hmangaina`, `nuomna`, `damna`, `hringna`).

---

### 3.2 What is *Na* in *Pang na*, *Ke na*, and *Lu na*? (Autonomous Stative Lexeme)
In expressions such as `pang na` or `ke na`, the word **`na` is NOT the nominalizing suffix `-na`**. 

Instead, it is an **autonomous lexical root** functioning as a **Stative Verb / Adjective / Noun** meaning **"pain", "to ache", "to hurt", "suffering", or "severe / painful"**:

| Expression | Component Morphemes | Exact Meaning | Grammatical Construction |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`ke na`** | `ke` (leg/foot) + `na` (aching / hurting) | **Leg pain / sore leg / hurting foot** | Noun + Stative Adjective / Predicate |
| **`pang na`** | `pang` (body) + `na` (aching / feverish) | **Body ache / illness / feverish body** | Noun + Stative Adjective / Predicate |
| **`lu na`** | `lu` (head) + `na` (aching) | **Headache / head ache** | Noun + Stative Adjective / Predicate |
| **`thinlung na`** | `thinlung` (heart) + `na` (hurting) | **Heartache / grief / deep sorrow** | Noun + Stative Adjective / Predicate |

- In spoken Hmar, these behave as regular predicative clauses:
  - *"Ka ke a na"* = *"My leg hurts."*
  - *"A pang a na"* = *"His body aches / He is unwell."*
  - *"Ka lu a na"* = *"My head aches."*
- When used attributively or as compound noun phrases, they remain **two distinct words** separated by a space: **`ke na`**, **`pang na`**, **`lu na`**.

---

### 3.3 Resolving the Apparent "Reversal" with *Luhai* vs. *Lu na*

A natural question arises:
> *"Why is dizziness fused as `luhai`, while plural heads is separated as `lu hai` — but for `na`, the symptom `lu na` is separated, while `hmangaina` is fused? Isn't the case of `na` reversed?"*

This is not a contradiction; it reflects the deep, consistent logic of Hmar grammar:

| Expression | Written Form | Why Fused or Separated? | Underlying Linguistic Principle |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Dizziness** | **`luhai`** (Fused) | **Compound Noun / Idiom**: `lu` (head) + `hai` (unsteady/spinning). It forms an indivisible unitary concept. | Single lexical item; `hai` here is not the plural marker. |
| **Heads (Plural)** | **`lu hai`** (Separated) | **Noun + Plural Marker**: `lu` (head) + `hai` (syntactic plural particle). | Free syntactic particle operating at the phrasal level. |
| **Love (Abstract)** | **`hmangaina`** (Fused) | **Verb + Bound Derivational Suffix**: `hmangai` (love) + `-na` (nominalizer). | Derivational affix creating an abstract noun. |
| **Headache** | **`lu na`** (Separated) | **Noun + Autonomous Stative Adjective/Verb**: `lu` (head) + `na` (aching). | Syntactic phrase / predication. `na` is an independent word. |
| **Body Ache / Illness** | **`pang na`** (Separated) | **Noun + Autonomous Stative Adjective/Verb**: `pang` (body) + `na` (aching). | Syntactic phrase. `na` is an independent word. |
| **Disease / Affliction** | **`natna`** (Fused) | **Root Verb + Derivational Suffix**: `nat` (to be ill) + `-na` (nominalizer). | When `na` itself is nominalized into an abstract noun, it fuses as `natna`. |

#### Summary of the Core Rule:
1. **Derivational affixes are FUSED:** `-na` nominalizing verbs (`hmangaina`, `nuomna`, `damna`, `natna`).
2. **Lexical compound roots are FUSED:** `luhai` (dizziness), `theihai` (mango), `panga` (five).
3. **Free grammatical particles are SEPARATED:** `hai` (plural), `ah` (locative), `a` (pronominal/locative).
4. **Autonomous lexical words in syntactic phrases are SEPARATED:** `lu na` (head hurts), `ke na` (foot hurts), `pang na` (body aches), `pang a` (on the body).

## 4. Linguistic & Grammatical Justification

### 4.1 Hmar is Typologically an Isolating / Analytic Language
Hmar belongs to the Tibeto-Burman language family (Kuki-Chin-Mizo sub-branch). Its nominal morphology is predominantly **isolating and analytic**, relying on autonomous grammatical particles rather than fusional synthetic declensions.
- Case and number markers (`hai`, `ah`, `in`, `chu`, `chun`, and clitic `a`) operate at the **phrasal level** (as clitics or independent postpositions), not as morphological word-level inflections.

### 4.2 Phrasal Cliticization Across Adjectival Modifiers
If `hai` were a bound nominal suffix, it would attach strictly to the head noun. However, in Hmar syntax, when an adjective modifies a noun, `hai` attaches to the **end of the entire noun phrase**:
- Head noun alone: `naupang hai` (*the children*)
- With adjective: `naupang fel hai` (*the well-behaved children*, **NOT** `*naupanghai fel`)
- With demonstrative / relative clause: `naupang fel tak hai chu`

Because `hai` floats to the boundary of the noun phrase, it functions syntactically as an **independent plural particle**, identical in autonomy to `chu`, `leh`, or `in`.

## 5. Master Rule Summary for the ISH Standard

1. **Plural Particle Rule:** Always write `hai` as a separate, space-delimited word when functioning as the plural marker.
   - Correct: `beram hai`, `thil hai`, `khawpui hai`, `mi hai`, `lekhabu hai`
   - Incorrect (Legacy Fused): `*beramhai`, `*thilhai`, `*khawpuihai`, `*mihai`, `*lekhabuhai`

2. **Compounds Containing Root `hai`:** Keep unitary compound nouns fused as single words.
   - `theihai` (mango)
   - `luhai` (dizziness / vertigo)

3. **Plural of Compounds:** Add the free particle `hai` after the compound noun.
   - `theihai hai` (mangoes)
   - `luhai hai` (plural of dizzy people)

4. **Numeral vs. Pronominal/Locative Clitic `a`:**
   - Unitary numeral 5: **`panga`** (always fused).
   - Anatomical/spatial phrase: **`pang a`** (body/side + `a`, strictly separated).

5. **Derivational Nominalizer `-na` vs. Autonomous Lexeme `na` (Pain):**
   - Abstract nominalizations formed from verbs: **`hmangaina`**, **`nuomna`**, **`damna`**, **`hringna`** (always fused).
   - Noun phrases denoting physical ache/hurting: **`lu na`**, **`ke na`**, **`pang na`** (always separated).
   - Nominalized illness/disease noun: **`natna`** (fused).

6. **Proper Names (Anthroponyms) vs. Syntactic Clitics:**
   - Male proper names derived with onomastic `-a`: **`Lawma`**, **`Rama`**, **`Thanga`**, **`Hlima`**, **`Runa`**, **`Fela`** (strictly TitleCase, preserved intact as proper nouns).
   - Syntactic phrases with clitic `a` in running text: **`lawm a`**, **`ram a`**, **`thang a`**, **`hlim a`**, **`run a`**, **`fel a`** (strictly separated with a space). Lowercase fused forms (`*lawma`, `*rama`) are pruned from the dictionary to eliminate homographic clash.
